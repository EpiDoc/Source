Line Beginnings: Direction of Text

2026-11-10

  • TEI definition: lb ; EpiDoc-specific customization: lb

As each line in an EpiDoc encoded inscription begins with an lb (see Line Beginnings), most attributes on this element should be understood to refer to the content of that line, i.e. all text before the following lb or the end of the containing ab, l, etc.

In the following text, we use “direction” to indicate the reading order of a line of text and “orientation” to indicate the layout in which the string or line of characters is displayed on the surface.

Text direction

The only case of text-direction defined in most versions of the Leiden System (e.g. Panciera and SEG) is that of Latin or Greek written in right-to-left direction, rendered with a left-facing arrow in the right margin.

SEG: abc ←

<ab>
 <lb style="direction:rtl"/>abc
<lb/>
</ab>

In most inscriptions, the reading direction and the direction in which the characters are facing are features of the language and writing system. Greek and Latin are usually read left-to-right; Arabic and Hebrew are usually read right-to-left and the characters have a conventional orientation. Some inscriptions are exceptions to this, but nonetheless well-defined, such as boustrophedon Greek inscriptions, in which odd-numbered lines are left-to-right, even-numbered right-to-left. If this is completely predictable or normal, then nothing special needs to be indicated in the Leiden edition nor in the EpiDoc markup.

Reading order and the facing of characters in a string are assumed to match. Individual characters that are reversed (or “retrograde”) may be indicated using hi. The EpiDoc Guidelines currently make no recommendation for encoding an entire line or text of retrograde characters, as none of the epigraphic traditions we know of indicate this in the text.

Where the direction or orientation of a line of text needs to be specified because it would not otherwise be clear, this information may be indicated in Leiden with an arrow in the right margin of the text; pointing left in the case of right-to-left text, or right in the case of unexpectedly left-to-right text. Examples might include a line of right-to-left Greek or Latin in an otherwise unidirectional text; two subsequent left-to-right lines in an otherwise regular boustrophedon; writing in dipinti on an archaic Greek vase, which annotates human figures in a variety of both directions and orientations. In all cases, the text would generally be transcribed according to the usual direction of the writing system used—left to right for Greek or Latin.

To explicitly record the non-standard direction of a line of writing in an inscription or other text, therefore, EpiDoc recommends the use of the style attribute on the lb immediately preceding the line of text in question. The value of style is based on the CSS class "direction" (although note that this is not intended as a processing instruction to render the text, but rather as a description of the source), with values "ltr" (left-to-right) or "rtl" (right-to-left), i.e. <lb style="direction:ltr"/>.

Text orientation

This section uses the term “orientation” to indicate the layout in which the string or line of characters is displayed on the surface, as in rotated text, vertical or diagonal writing, or lines written in a circle, spiral, etc. This is distinct from the reading direction of individual lines, and therefore may be encoded in association with text direction—but either may be indicated independently as appropriate.

A few common text orientations are defined in these Guidelines, as required by epigraphic disciplines including dipinti on Greek vases, Egyptian Hieroglyphs, Ogham and Etruscan.

To explicitly record the orientation of a line of text, EpiDoc again recommends the style attribute on the lb preceding the line, containing the CSS class “writing-mode” (used as a descriptor, not a processing instruction), with values such as “vertical” (line rotated so text is written top to bottom); “verticalup” (line rotates so text is written bottom to top); “diagonalnw” (text written bottom-right to top-left); “diagonalne”, “diagonalse”, “diagonalsw”. Values such as “circlecw”, “circleccw” and “spiralcw”, “spiralccw” are also proposed, but not yet implemented. A fuller list is given in the table below.

If you need more variations of these or other types of orientation, feel free to follow this model and invent them, bearing in mind you will also need to customise the Reference Stylesheets to render any arrows or similar. As with direction, orientation need only be recorded if it is relevant to your edition, e.g. in line with unusual, unexpected or unpredictable orientations.
